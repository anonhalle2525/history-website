Hellenistic Culture:The Advanced Mathematics and Science

Elisa, Lily, Haeum, Steve, and Brandon

Historical question

How did the illumination of mathematics and science transform the initial ideas and reform the life of people during the Hellenistic period?

Our claim

New discoveries in mathematics and science updated the original concepts and reformed people’s lives during the Hellenistic period. They changed undetermined hypotheses into verified theories that gave logical explanations of universal knowledge, and they remain influential in the study of mathematics and science today.

Start with the historical context

Historical context

Before Alexander’s conquests, myth explained much of the world. Afterward, Greek learning met knowledge from Egypt, Persia, and other cultures, and calculation began to replace legend.

Page by Elisa

Before the innovation

Before the Hellenistic period, people relied on myths to explain the earth, time, health, and nature. This left daily life unsettled, because myths were not always correct or accurate.

After Alexander’s conquests, Hellenistic scientific development changed that approach by improving science and technology. People began to explain things using science, and Greek culture mixed with knowledge from Egypt, Persia, and other cultures.

The effect of the innovation

As discoveries about the universe and mathematics grew, people could rely on calculated results and repeatable patterns instead of myths and legends that could not be trusted every time.

The Hellenistic period is widely known for the growth of new innovations that made daily life easier.

Before and after

Predicting the seasons

Before

Farmers relied on the myth of Demeter to predict the seasons.

After

Agriculture could expand because of the mathematical calendars made by Meton and Hipparchus.

Travel and maps

Before

People were afraid to travel far, unsure what lay beyond the boundaries of the known world.

After

Eratosthenes worked out the Earth’s circumference using the geometry of shadows, which allowed people to make maps, grids, and navigation tools.

Timeline

  1. c. 432 BCE

    Meton of Athens proposes a 19-year calendar cycle.

  2. 334–323 BCE

    Alexander’s campaigns spread Greek rule and learning across Egypt and Persia. The Hellenistic period is usually dated from his death in 323 BCE.

  3. c. 300 BCE

    Euclid organizes earlier mathematics in the Elements.

  4. c. 240 BCE

    Eratosthenes calculates the Earth’s circumference.

  5. 212 BCE

    Archimedes dies when Rome captures Syracuse.

  6. Mid-100s BCE

    Hipparchus works on calendar cycles and astronomical tables.

The innovation: the phalanx

Alexander’s army fought in the Macedonian phalanx, a formation developed in the military reforms of his father, Philip II.

Page by Steve

How it worked

The phalanx was a large square block of soldiers, usually 16 men wide and 16 deep, standing shoulder to shoulder. Depending on the situation it could be smaller, around 8 by 8, or larger, up to 32 by 32.

Each man carried a sarissa, a pike about 4 to 6 meters long. The front rows lowered their pikes to make a wall of sharp points, while the rows behind held theirs upward to block arrows, and the whole formation pushed ahead. Each man also carried a small shield and a short sword as backup.

Try the formation

  • First four ranks: sarissas lowered toward the enemy
  • Rear ranks: sarissas held upward against arrows

Who fought where

Phalanx
The main block, armed with the long sarissa.
Hypaspists
Elite troops with shorter spears who linked the phalanx to the cavalry.
Cavalry
Fought with lances and curved swords.
Light troops
Archers and javelin throwers who went in front to start the fight.
Reserves
Waited behind to guard against being surrounded.

Evidence

“The sarissa was a perfect weapon for the pezhetairoi, for it extended their reach advantage over the spears of the enemy.”

Wasson, World History Encyclopedia, “Pezhetairoi.”

Source: World History Encyclopedia, “Pezhetairoi.” It covers the military reforms of Philip II, the Battle of Issus, and the Battle of Gaugamela.

Different perspectives

Soldiers, engineers, scientists, and mathematicians each used Hellenistic discoveries in their own way.

Page by Lily

Military forces

Archimedes’ demonstrations of engineering led to the invention of war machines. They helped military forces and advanced the understanding of mechanics.

“According to Plutarch’s Life of Marcellus… Archimedes reportedly used a system of compound pulleys to move a heavily loaded ship with surprisingly little effort. This demonstration impressed the king so much that he asked Archimedes to prepare defensive and offensive machines for siege warfare… suggests that his military engineering grew out of a much broader understanding of mechanics.”

Myth and Memory (secondary)

Primary source: Plutarch, Life of Marcellus

Scientists and engineers

Scientists could build on Archimedes’ proposals to create innovative machines, adding to the history of invention.

“Using Archimedes’ ideas, Hellenistic scientists later built a force pump, pneumatic machines, and even a steam engine.”

Textbook (secondary)

Primary source: Hero of Alexandria, Heronis Alexandrini Spiritalium liber

Hellenistic scientists

Scientists like Eratosthenes and Archimedes made their own successful attempts to explore astronomy and science, using the ideas Euclid had found to build innovative studies.

“The influence of Euclid on later scientists such as Archimedes, Apollonius of Perga, Galileo Galilei, Sir Isaac Newton, and Christiaan Huygens was immense. Eratosthenes used his theorems to measure with surprising accuracy the size of the sphere of Earth, and Aristarchus attempted less successfully, but in fine Euclidean style, to establish the sizes and distances of the moon and the sun.”

EBSCO Research Starters, “Euclid” (secondary)

Primary source: Euclid, The Thirteen Books of Euclid’s “Elements,” translated by Thomas Little Heath. Volume 1, commentary notes for Book I, Proposition 29; Introduction, Chapter II; and Preface.

Hellenistic mathematicians

Many Hellenistic mathematicians kept revising mathematics based on Euclid’s discoveries, adding new findings to his work. This improved the field overall and helped later mathematicians make and learn from new discoveries.

“Other Hellenistic mathematicians, such as Hero of Alexandria, Pappus, Simplicius, and, most important, Proclus, produced commentaries on the Elements. Theon of Alexandria, father of the famous woman philosopher and mathematician Hypatia, introduced a new edition of the Elements in the fourth century C.E.”

EBSCO Research Starters, “Euclid” (secondary)

Primary source: Euclid, The Thirteen Books of Euclid’s “Elements,” translated by Thomas Little Heath. Volume 1, Introduction, Chapter III.

How this supports our claim

This evidence shows how influential Hellenistic discoveries in mathematics and science were across many positions in society. It supports our claim that those discoveries changed the original concepts and the lives of many different people.

Short-term and long-term effects

New ideas first improved daily tools and calendars. Over time they became the foundation for Roman and later scholarship.

Page by Brandon

Short term

Scholars made new discoveries in mathematics, astronomy, science, art, and engineering. These ideas helped people create better calendars, machines, and scientific ideas for understanding nature.

Long term

Hellenistic mathematics and science influenced Roman scholars. This strong base supported the growth of future ideas, and the work of Euclid and Archimedes became an important foundation for later mathematics, astronomy, and engineering.

Final verdict

Page by Lily

Our question

How did the illumination of mathematics and science transform the initial ideas and reform the life of people during the Hellenistic period?

Our judgment

The illumination of mathematics and science in the Hellenistic period reformed diverse people’s lives by offering logical, verified reasoning in place of undetermined myths, and by inspiring further discoveries and studies.

For instance, many social positions were affected, including farmers, military forces, travelers, engineers, scientists, and mathematicians. Euclid built the foundation of geometry, Archimedes gave innovative demonstrations and explanations in engineering and mechanics, and Eratosthenes successfully estimated the size of the Earth.

These innovations supported engineering and mechanics used for agricultural, navigational, and military purposes. They also helped scientists, astronomers, and mathematicians study more accurately and precisely.
